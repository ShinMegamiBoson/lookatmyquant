At 1.2 GB our Qwen3.5-4B solves 330 of 540 math problems and Bonsai 2 Ternary solves 328

Bonsai 2 Ternary 4B [1] is Prism ML's ternary-weight version of Qwen3.5-4B [2]. It stores 2-bit codes with a scale and a bias per 128 weights, and its language-model weights take 1.20 GB. The original's take 8.4 GB in bf16.

Our file is the same model at 2.3 bits per weight, 1.23 GB. It is post-training quantization with trellis codes from the LookAtMyQuant compiler, followed by 4,000 steps of distillation from the original.

The math test is 540 generated problems with exact integer or fraction answers, in twelve families at three difficulties, from long addition to determinants and modular exponentiation. Scoring is exact match on the final boxed answer. Decoding is greedy with thinking disabled and at most 2,048 new tokens.

Ours solves 330, Bonsai 328 and the original 359. 62 problems are solved only by ours and 60 only by Bonsai (McNemar p = 0.93), so the test does not separate the two files.

On text, ours is closer to the original. KL(original ‖ model) on held-out text from 13 sources is 0.45 nats for ours and 0.59 for Bonsai, and ours is lower on 11 of the 13 (Figure 2). Bonsai is lower on chat and Hindi.

All sizes are the language model alone. Qwen3.5-4B also has a 0.67 GB vision tower, which Bonsai's 1.87 GB download keeps in bf16. Our file omits it, and we did not evaluate image inputs.

A 4B model at 2.3 bits beats a 2B at 5 bits and a 0.8B at 8 bits

The same space can hold a smaller model at a higher bit rate. We quantized the two smaller Qwen3.5 models the way MLX's standard files do [8]: affine quantization in groups of 64 weights, each with a 16-bit scale and bias, which costs N + 0.5 bits per weight at N bits.

Qwen3.5-2B at 5 bits is 1.29 GB and solves 284. At 4 bits it is 1.06 GB and solves 242. Qwen3.5-0.8B at 8 bits is 0.80 GB and solves 205; more bits would not help it, since its relative weight error at 8 bits is already 0.5%. Their KL from the original 4B on text is 0.51, 0.60 and 0.75, against 0.45 for ours.

At 2.3 bits, KL from the original does not predict exact-answer math

A scalar Lloyd–Max build at this size, calibrated on generic text, has WikiText-2 KL 0.60. Bonsai's is 0.50. The build solves 1 problem of 540 and Bonsai solves 328.

The two metrics also separate in the other direction. Our post-training build already solves 317 at a KL of 1.41 on the 13 sources. Fine-tuning then cuts that KL to 0.45 and adds 13 problems. So we selected every component below on the benchmark and report KL beside it. Figure 3 adds the components one at a time.

Calibrating on the model's own long worked solutions, with digit tokens weighted 10×, nearly doubled the score at fixed codes

Layer-wise quantization minimizes error under the input second moment H = E[w·xxᵀ], so the calibration set and the token weights w decide which errors count. With trellis codes fixed and half of the set the original's GSM8K solutions [10], the build solves 117.

An all-math set with w = 1 at positions that predict a digit and w = 0.1 elsewhere gives 147. Replacing 256 sequences of 512 tokens with 64 of 2,048, the original's greedy solutions to MATH training problems [9], gives 224. These have long chains of intermediate results, like the benchmark's.

Bits belong to rows: 10% of a matrix's rows can carry 79% of its Fisher information on digits

For each output row i we measure si = E[gi²]·E[yi²] on the original model. Here y is the row's output and g is the gradient of the log-likelihood of tokens sampled from the model's own predictions at digit-predicting positions. In the matrices that read a linear-attention layer's input, 10% of the rows carry 79% of the total.

A global allocation of 1 to 4 bits per row group, across all matrices and under the same byte budget, took the score from 265 to 300. It puts 13% of the matrix weights at 1 bit, 47% at 2, 36% at 3 and 4% at 4. Rotating the output side as well would average this structure away, so the incoherence transform is on the input side only.

e-means codebooks cut trellis MSE by 4% to 9% at the same bytes and added 17 problems

A trellis code decodes each weight from a 4,096-entry codebook. We had fit it with six full-batch Lloyd iterations on a fixed set of Gaussian sequences. e-means [7] replaces full passes with mini-batch updates through exponential moving averages. Fit that way, with fresh Gaussian samples in every batch, an EMA of the sum and count for each codeword, and a batch weight on a cosine schedule from 0.5 to 0.01, the codebook's MSE under the deployed encoder falls 4.0%, 4.5%, 6.2% and 8.8% at 1 to 4 bits.

The file size and the decoder do not change. The score went from 300 to 317, and KL on the 13 sources fell 4%. With e-means codebooks at 2 and 3 bits only, the score was 311.

Error propagation at half strength beat full strength by 41 problems

QEP [6] adds a cross term to the layer objective, so that the quantized layer on quantized inputs matches the original layer on clean inputs, with a strength α. At α = 1 the build solves 224. At α = 0.5 it solves 265, and WikiText-2 KL falls from 0.85 to 0.66.

Fine-tuning leaves trellis codes where they are; re-quantizing from the original weights mid-training cut KL on text by another 43%

Our fine-tuning is distillation on latent full-precision weights W that are pulled toward their quantized values P and re-quantized every 1,000 steps (Figure 5). It took the score from 317 to 321 and KL on the 13 sources from 1.41 to 0.79. The codes hardly moved: 0.1% of a layer's codes change at a re-quantization, because a weight held near its quantized value quantizes back to the same code.

So between steps 1,000 and 1,750, each layer is re-quantized once from the original weights W0 in place of W. H comes from an exponential moving average over the training batches' own activations, about 2,000 sequences of the partly fine-tuned model, eight times the build's calibration tokens. 97% of the codes change, W restarts at the new P, and training continues.

On two builds this gave 330 and 324 problems at KL 0.45 and 0.45, against 321 and 314 at 0.79 and 0.80 without it (Table 2). Part of the gain on text comes from the data mix: the running H is 30% non-math text, and the build's calibration set is all math.

The recipe is post-training quantization aimed at digits, then distillation that ends exactly on the codes

Table 3. The final recipe.

ComponentSetting
Post-training quantization
CodesTrellis-coded quantization as in QTIP [3]: a bitshift trellis with L = 12 and V = 1, at 1 to 4 bits per weight, decoded from a 4,096-entry codebook per bit rate. Codebooks are fit with e-means.
BasisA randomized Hadamard transform on each matrix's input side [4], shared by the matrices that read the same activations. One scale per row.
RoundingViterbi along each row, 16 columns at a time with 8 of look-ahead, with LDLQ error feedback [5] under H = E[w·xxᵀ].
Calibration64 sequences of 2,048 tokens: the original's greedy solutions to MATH training problems. w = 1 at digit-predicting positions and 0.1 elsewhere.
Error propagationQEP [6] at α = 0.5.
Bit allocationPer-matrix rates from the compiler, then a global allocation over 1,520 row groups in 190 matrices by Fisher information on digits. The 200 matrices average 2.32 bits per weight.
EmbeddingThe tied embedding, 636M weights, in frequency buckets at 1 to 8 bits, 1.88 on average.
Fine-tuning, 4,000 steps
StudentLatent full-precision weights W, initialized at the dequantized build. P is W's current quantized value.
LossKL(original ‖ student) over the original's top 64 tokens plus one bucket for the tail, at 128 positions of each 512-token sequence, with digit-predicting positions weighted 10×. Batches of 8, half math and half general text, science, code, instructions and tool calls. The math is the original's own solutions to GSM8K and MATH training problems and, for about an eighth of it, to problems from the benchmark's generator at another seed, with wrong calculations corrected and only right answers kept. Every test question is excluded by hash.
OptimizerAdam, learning rate 10⁻⁵, with ε set per tensor to its initial gradient RMS. Norm weights and the embedding's scales and codebooks train as well.
PullAfter each step, W ← W + β(P − W) with β = 10⁻³. Over the last 600 steps β rises to 0.03 and the learning rate falls to 0. Then W ← P.
Re-quantizationEvery 250 steps, P ← Q(W) for a quarter of the layers with the build's own quantizer, and row scales and codebooks are refit. Between steps 1,000 and 1,750, each layer's P ← Q(W0) once, under a running H.

The latent weights are there because a trellis-coded weight has no small step: one path bit changes the decoded values of the weights after it. Quantizing one layer takes 20 to 40 seconds, so a straight-through estimator that re-quantizes at every step is out of reach. The pull has to be strong. A run with β = 3·10⁻⁴, and re-quantization twice as often, left the weights nearly twice as far from their codes and lost the gain on math.

The compiler now allocates bits to rows against a digit-weighted objective, and fine-tunes after allocation

LookAtMyQuant 1 measured the KL that each stored piece of a model costs when it alone is compressed, and chose one code per piece under a byte budget. It stopped at post-training quantization and listed fine-tuning after allocation as further work. Table 4 lists what this recipe changes. The code for all of it is in the public repository, with a job for each step.

Limitations

  • Each file is a single run. Builds of one recipe have differed by 31 of 540 problems, so a difference of a few problems is noise.
  • The 540 was our development set, which favors our files and not Bonsai. On a second set of 216 problems from the same generator, which we used less, our file solves 125 and Bonsai 138.
  • The calibration and distillation data are mostly math, and about an eighth of the fine-tuning's math text comes from the benchmark's own generator. The post-training build's calibration set does not. Beyond KL on text, we have not evaluated coding, tool use or instruction following.
  • Our language-model weights are 1.23 GB packed, 2% more than Bonsai's 1.20 GB. That is the packed size of a build with the same layout. New codebooks and fine-tuning change the values a file stores and leave its layout alone, and we did not re-pack the fine-tuned file.
  • All scores are from the dequantized weights in fp32. We have not measured decode speed for this file.
