Adding a Neural Prosthetic to a Language Model Without Harming It

Five cards, small add-ons that run beside Qwen3.5-4B and leave its weights untouched, each give it one exact tool: arithmetic and algebra, strings, calendars and units, bits and networks, and logic and graphs. With a gate each, all five run on the model at once (27.0 MB). On their own held-out problems they score 93.8% against the plain model’s 50.7%, none of its answers to 4,457 standard questions changes, and on four public benchmarks they never trained on they score 81.8% against 49.8%.

Tool fine-tuning buys capability by rewriting the base model

Language models fail reliably at exact work: multi-digit arithmetic and algebra, counting the letters in a word, adding days to a date, operating on bits, solving a graph or logic puzzle. The standard remedy, fine-tuning the model’s weights to call tools or write programs [1] [2] [3] [4], raises scores but silently edits every layer of the base network. Published tool papers rarely measure paired degradation on general benchmarks against the exact parent checkpoint, or they rely on aggregate averages where replay on code or QA masks general-knowledge forgetting and instruction-following collapse.

We ask a stricter question: can a language model gain exact tools as a Pareto improvement: far better at what each tool does, statistically indistinguishable from the untouched model on everything else, and able to run on top of an already-quantized model at nearly its native speed?

Our answer builds on neuron-homology maps [5] and cross-scale circuit grafting [6]. Instead of updating the host’s weights, we attach cards, small neural prostheses (Figure 1). A card is a sidecar network that re-runs a seat of the host’s own MLP neurons beside the host, a gate that decides from the host’s activations whether a prompt is for this card, and an exact engine that types results into the reply. We built five: arithmetic and algebra (with SymPy), strings, calendars and units, bits and networks, and logic and graphs (with Z3 and graph algorithms). Every weight of the host stays bit-for-bit frozen, verified by checksum.

A card re-runs its own seat of the model’s neurons beside the model and answers only when the model calls its tool

At each layer l of Qwen3.5-4B [7], the host computes its normal residual update untouched. In parallel, a card reads the host’s clean residual stream xclean = x − δH through a trained low-rank read adapter, runs the frozen slice of its seat’s neurons with learned per-neuron gain α and soft-threshold τ, accumulates its state in a private stream sD, and writes back through a zero-initialized low-rank write adapter:

ul = RMSNorml( xclean + (xclean − μl) UA,lVA,lT + sD )
vl = soft_shrink(fcalc,l(ul) − ml; τl) ⊙ αl · Wdown,calc,l,   sD ← sD + vl
ΔH,l = βl vl + vl UB,lVB,lT,   δH ← δH + ΔH,l
  • Nothing is copied. A card reads its seat’s neurons from the host’s own weights, 16-bit or quantized, and stores only its adapters, per-neuron gains and thresholds and gate: 5.4 MB at rank 4.
  • Two checks decide when a card acts. A prompt gate (504K parameters) reads the untouched host at layers 11, 21 and 31 on the prompt’s last token and attaches the card for that reply only if the prompt is of its kind. Inside the reply, at each “=”, the card’s own stream must say the model is calling the tool and a strict parser must accept the expression before it; only then does the engine type in the result. Numbers in a line to be copied, and equations such as x2 − 4 = 0, are left alone.
  • Training anchored to the plain model. A card learns to call its tool on verified examples and to ignore hard negatives while matching the plain model’s predictions on held-out prose; the arithmetic card also trains on the model’s own verified GSM8K and MATH replies, with the engine’s digits masked so that reasoning length and style stay the same. Both anchors are needed: trained on terse worked examples instead, the arithmetic card made the model shorten its GSM8K reasoning from 404 to 103 tokens and fall from 92.0% to 81.5%, and an ordinary fine-tune (LoRA [8]) of the same size, trained the same way, moved its predictions on prose 2.75× as far as the card did.

With a gate each, all five cards share one model, and none of its 4,457 standard answers changes

Each card was tested on held-out problems of its kind: 740 for arithmetic and algebra (180 exact arithmetic problems in twelve families, 150 word problems with large numbers, 200 from GSM8K [9], 150 from MATH [10] and 60 computer-algebra problems) and 120 from six generated families for each of the other four, plus 80 lines that contain sums or tool calls and that the model is asked to copy exactly. We also tested on 4,457 standard questions from five benchmarks (Figure 3, Table 1).

Attached together and always on, the cards hurt each other: arithmetic and algebra fell from 88.0% with that card alone to 74.9%, and the other four from 99.4% to 80.6%, because every card adds its correction to the residual stream on every token, whatever the prompt. The prompt gates fix this. Each gate reads the model with no card attached, so it decides the same way whatever other cards are installed.

With all five cards and their gates installed (27.0 MB, at rank 4, on seats that share no neuron), no card attached on any of the 14,356 standard-benchmark sequences (each multiple-choice option is scored as its own sequence) or the 32 prose passages, so the model answered all 4,457 standard questions exactly as before. Each card attached on all of its own problems. Arithmetic and algebra scored 90.0% against the plain model’s 65.7%, and the other four 478 of 480 (99.6%) against 27.5%; most of the plain model’s misses on those four are replies cut off at the 1,024-token limit (317 of 480). All 80 lines were copied exactly, and on every row the stack scored exactly what each card scored alone.

On four public benchmarks they never saw, the cards score 81.8% against 49.8%

The cards above were trained on problems in our own formats. Working on problems written by others took two more steps. The engines had to accept the calls a model writes for public problems (graphs with two-way edges, several letters in one count, unit spellings such as “kW·h”, calls longer than 700 characters), and the gate had to read the end of a long prompt rather than only its first 768 tokens. Then the strings, calendar, bits and logic cards were trained again on the same seats and at the same size, with three more problem families each written in the formats of ten public benchmarks (the “v2” cards). Without these steps the five cards gained 4.8 points on the ten benchmarks, almost all of it on graph maximum flow.

With them, the stack scores 83.5% on those ten benchmarks against the intact model’s 61.7% (Table 2). That is not a measure of transfer, because the new training problems reuse the benchmarks’ own wording: with numbers, quoted strings and names masked, every six-word phrase of an NLGraph prompt also appears in a training prompt, as do 80% of a letter-counting prompt’s and 32% of a logic puzzle’s.

To test transfer we built 850 held-out problems (Table 3). 450 are problems from four of the ten benchmarks rewritten with the same facts and answers: a graph becomes a road map, a pipe network or an adjacency list, and a logic puzzle a race, a row of books or a set of prices. Every rewritten problem was solved again from its parsed facts, and none shares more than 6% of its six-word phrases with training. The other 400 come from four public benchmarks no part of this work had used: GraphWiz shortest paths and maximum flow [12], which share at most 14%, and letter counts from oumi [13] and Natural Instructions task 113 [14], which share none. The intact model and the stack ran with the same harness and the same 1,024-token limit.

On the four unseen benchmarks (Figure 4) the stack scores 81.8% against the intact model’s 49.8% (131 gained, 3 lost, p = 3.7 × 10−35). Rewording costs the v2 cards at most 2 points on graphs and letter counting (shortest paths 100% → 99%, maximum flow 98% → 96%, letter counts 99% → 98%), and they call their engine on at least 98 of the 100 problems in each set, so these skills do not depend on the training wording (Figure 4, second panel). The logical-deduction gain does: reworded, the cards call the solver on 17 of 150 puzzles and score 25.3% against 62.0% in the original wording. The rewritten puzzles also take longer to reason through: the intact model ran out of tokens on 140 of 150, so its 6.7% there is not a fair baseline. On GraphWiz shortest paths all 45 engine calls were right and all 22 graphs of up to 20 nodes were solved; every miss is a larger graph whose edge list, up to 216 edges, did not fit in the 1,024 tokens while the model copied it into the call. Of the stack’s 21.7 points on the ten benchmarks, the 2.7 from logical deduction depend on the training wording, and the gains on graphs and letter counting, which account for the rest, carry over.

The v2 cards keep the stack’s preservation. No gate opened on any of the 14,042 MMLU, 1,172 ARC-Challenge, 500 HellaSwag or 1,221 CommonsenseQA questions; no answer changed on the last three or on HumanEval, where a gate opened on 2 of 164 problems; the arithmetic card still scores 90.0% on its problems; the v2 cards score 99.0% on their 480; and all 80 copy lines stay verbatim.

Four published calculator and math-tool fine-tunes all churn 15%–34% of standard answers and collapse to 0% on copy controls

Published tool fine-tunes exist for arithmetic, so the arithmetic card is the one we can set beside them, here on its own at rank 8 (7.04 MB). We ran four published calculator and math-tool fine-tunes against their own parent models on the same 4,457 standard questions: MMLU [23] (1,400), ARC-Challenge [24] (1,172), HellaSwag [25] (500), CommonsenseQA [26] (1,221) and HumanEval [27] (164). We also measured each on 32 prose passages and on the same 780 math, algebra and copy problems, each with its official prompt and live SymPy or Python interpreter (Tables 4 and 5). ToRA-Code-7B [3] ran with the rotary-position base it was trained with (10,000, not the 1,000,000 left in its configuration), without which its scores collapse.

Every one of them changes the model it is built on. On the standard questions Calcformer-FLAN-XL [2] moves −15.02 points (p = 1.1 × 10−74) and Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct [7] −20.78 (p = 2.8 × 10−136); OpenMath-Mistral-7B [4] moves −2.43 points on MMLU (p = 0.03); and ToRA-Code-7B gains overall (+1.68 points) but loses HellaSwag (−3.2) and answers 18.3% of the questions differently. All four solve the sums inside lines they were told to copy and copy none of the 40, where the arithmetic card copies all 40. On the 740 math and algebra problems the arithmetic card on the 4B model scores 88.0%, above OpenMath-Mistral-7B (80.3%) and ToRA-Code-7B (77.0%) and 2.8 points below the 7B Qwen2.5-Math (90.8%).

On a 1.22 GB quantized host, the arithmetic card beats the unquantized 8.41 GB model and heals non-math benchmarks

Quantizing Qwen3.5-4B to 2.32 bits per weight (1.22 GB with LookAtMyQuant 2 [28]) makes it 6.8× smaller but costs accuracy: math and algebra fall from 65.7% to 63.6% and the 4,457 standard questions from 62.84% to 53.31%. We attach the arithmetic card to the frozen quantized model, reading its seat’s neurons from the quantized weights, and train it for 1,000 steps to match the full 16-bit model’s predictions on prose (Figure 6). The 1.229 GB result scores 83.9% on math and algebra, 18.2 points above the full 8.41 GB model (p = 6.1 × 10−20), copies 100.0% of the copy lines exactly, and gains +1.66 points on the standard questions (+74 problems net, p = 2.4 × 10−5), most on HumanEval (+7.3 points) and HellaSwag (+5.0), with MMLU unchanged (−0.14).

A card runs at about 91.1% of the model’s speed, and the arithmetic card solves hard math up to 8.0× faster

A fine-tune that edits the weights runs at its parent’s speed. A card adds a small computation at every layer, which matters whenever the model stays frozen or quantized and an adapter cannot be merged into its weights.

We timed the arithmetic card, on its own at rank 8, against its untouched parent on an H100, warm and in counterbalanced order (Figure 7, Table 6). It runs at 91.1% of the model’s speed reading 4,293 multiple-choice questions, 90.4% on HumanEval, and 75.2% and 79.1% writing fixed-length replies one and sixteen at a time. An unmerged LoRA of the same size runs at 96.7% and 92.2%, and putting a 325-token tool description in every prompt instead cuts multiple-choice speed to 17.0%. On hard problems it is faster than the model alone: typing exact results in shortens replies 5–10× (746 → 69 tokens on algebra), so it solves exact arithmetic 1.2× and symbolic algebra 8.0× faster end to end (3.5× on the quantized model).

Limitations

  • Single host family. All cards were trained on Qwen3.5-4B (16-bit BF16 and 2.32-bit quantized). Generalizing the same mapped-neuron sidecar to other model families and larger scales remains open.
  • Single seeds. Each arm is a single training run; p-values reflect paired variation across problems, not across seeds.
  • Untooled base-model denominators vary across published pairs. Raw base checkpoints (Mistral-7B, CodeLlama-7B-Python) start much lower on math than Qwen3.5-4B and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (0%–15% vs. 66%–70% pooled), so Table 5 reports raw parent and tool accuracy rather than a ratio, which a near-zero parent score inflates.
  • Generated tool suites and benchmark formats. The four new tool suites are generated from six problem families each and held out from training, and the intact model’s score on them is capped by the 1,024-token reply limit it hit on 317 of 480 problems, so they measure what the cards add at that budget. The v2 cards were trained on the formats of the ten public benchmarks, so the evidence of transfer is Figure 4 and Table 3, which cover graphs and letter counting but no unseen logic-puzzle benchmark. Every comparison is with the model without tools; we did not compare against the same model fine-tuned on the same problems with the same engines.
  • Simulated BF16 dequantization on H100 for the quantized host. The 1.22 GB quantized weights were decoded faithfully from the real 2.32-bit packed artifact into PyTorch BF16 for GPU training and benchmarking; fusing a card into a native packed Apple Silicon MLX or CUDA trellis kernel is the next deployment step.
